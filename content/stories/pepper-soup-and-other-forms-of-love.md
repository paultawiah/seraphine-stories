My grandmother spoke love in a dialect I spent twenty years learning to translate.

Not English. Not Twi, exactly. Something older and more practical — a language made of actions, of pots on fire at five in the morning, of the specific sound her slippers made on concrete as she walked to check on you in the night when she thought you were sleeping.

She never said *I love you*. Not once, not to me, not to anyone I witnessed. I used to think this was a deficiency. Now I think she was simply fluent in a language I was too young to read.

---

The first time I understood, I was eight years old and sick with fever.

I remember the ceiling of the room she kept for me at her house in Kumasi. Water stains on the plaster, a fan that made a clicking sound on every third rotation. I was too hot and then too cold and the world kept tilting in a way that made me feel seasick, though I had never been near the sea.

She came in without knocking. She always did.

She sat on the edge of the bed — I felt the mattress dip, the specific weight of her — and pressed one palm to my forehead. She didn't say anything. She clicked her tongue once, the way she did when she was assessing a situation and finding it unacceptable.

Then she went away.

Twenty minutes later she came back with pepper soup.

Not the kind from a packet. Proper pepper soup: the broth dark and complex, the goat meat soft enough to fall apart, the heat hitting the back of your throat in a way that seemed to physically push the illness out of you. She fed it to me herself, one careful spoon at a time, not speaking.

By the time the bowl was empty, my fever had broken. I believe this was not coincidence.

---

She had a system.

If you arrived at her house looking tired, she made you sit down and put food in front of you before she allowed you to explain yourself. *Tell me what happened* was always the second question. The first was always something she said with her hands — the placement of a cup of tea, the angle of a chair she'd positioned to face the light.

If you arrived looking heartbroken, she asked no questions at all. She simply cooked something that took a long time. The longer the cooking, the more serious she considered the situation. Ordinary sadness earned you rice and stew. A broken engagement once produced a full Sunday soup that simmered from morning to evening, and my aunt who had suffered the engagement sat in the kitchen the whole time, watching the pot, and something passed between them that I was too young to understand but old enough to feel.

---

I thought about her system when I fell in love for the first time.

His name was Daniel. He was terrible for me in ways that took me three years to fully catalogue, but for a long time I confused his dramatic declarations with depth. He said *I love you* constantly. He said it in messages, in arguments, in the middle of sentences where it didn't quite fit, like a word he'd heard was powerful and was testing to see if its power would work on me.

It did, for a while.

Then one night I was sick — not dramatically sick, just the ordinary sick of someone who has pushed themselves too far for too long — and he texted me he was sorry to hear it and hoped I felt better soon, and then he went out with his friends.

And I lay there in my flat in Accra, sweating and slightly feverish, thinking about the ceiling of my grandmother's room in Kumasi. About a fan that clicked every third rotation. About pepper soup made from scratch at some hour that most people consider the middle of the night.

I understood something then. About the difference between love as a declaration and love as a practice. About how easy it is to say the word and how demanding it is to make the soup.

---

We broke up four months later. He said I was cold and unappreciative. I think what he meant was that I had finally learned to tell the difference between someone performing love and someone doing it.

---

My grandmother died when I was twenty-six.

I flew to Kumasi for the funeral and helped the aunties cook the funeral food and tried not to think about all the Thursdays I had not visited when I should have, when there had been time, when she was still there to put food in front of me before asking her second question.

In her kitchen, going through her things, I found a notebook. Not a diary — she wasn't that kind of woman. More of a household record: prices of things, recipes, names of reliable tradespeople. The kind of document that is a love letter to an ordinary life.

On one page, in her careful handwriting, she had written a list of names. Mine was on it. Next to my name, she had written a single note: *pepper soup — extra pepper, no uziza, she doesn't like the bitterness.*

She had known. She had always known how I took it. She had adjusted accordingly for years, maybe decades, without ever mentioning it, without expecting acknowledgment.

Just the small permanent work of paying attention.

---

I cook now. I have learned that this is not incidental.

When someone I love is sick, I make soup from scratch. When someone I love is heartbroken, I make something that takes a long time. When someone I love arrives at my door looking tired, I tell them to sit and I put something in front of them before I ask my questions.

People who don't know me sometimes think I'm being old-fashioned. People who know me understand that I am being precise.

There is a grammar to love that goes beyond what language can hold. My grandmother was fluent in it. She taught me, slowly, in a language made of early mornings and adjusted recipes and a fan that clicked every third rotation.

*I love you* is four words.

Extra pepper, no uziza — she doesn't like the bitterness — is an entire education.
