The phone kept ringing even after they had lowered the casket.

Ama stood at the edge of the grave in her black cloth, her mother's hand on her shoulder, and felt the vibration in her pocket like a small heart refusing to stop. She did not look at it. She stared at the mahogany box and tried to remember the last real thing Kweku had said to her — not the last message, not the last missed call, but the last *sound* of him.

She couldn't.

---

They had been what her friends called "the complicated kind." Not broken, not whole. Somewhere in the geography between those two states, where you still call each other on bad days but sleep on opposite sides of the bed when you're in the same city, which wasn't often.

Kweku worked in Kumasi. She was in Accra. The distance was three hours by road and several years by heart.

"We're fine," she used to say, when her mother asked. "We just have different rhythms."

What she meant was: *I love him in theory and he loves me in practice and neither of us knows how to bridge that.*

---

He died on a Thursday. Car accident on the Kumasi-Accra road, the kind that the news reports in a single sentence and the families spend decades recovering from. He was coming to see her. She learned this detail last, from his sister, almost as an afterthought.

*He was on his way to surprise you.*

She had to sit down when she heard that. She sat on the kitchen floor and didn't move for a long time.

---

It was her cousin who found the messages.

"Ama." Adwoa sat beside her on the bed three days after the funeral, phone in hand, face doing that thing faces do when they are carrying something too heavy for words. "Did you know he had been writing to you?"

She hadn't. Or rather — she had seen the notifications piling up in the weeks before, but she had been angry about something she could no longer name, and she had let them sit there, unread, a small passive punishment she'd been administering to both of them.

There were forty-seven messages. Starting from the first of October and ending the morning he left Kumasi.

She read them all in one sitting, alone, with the door locked and the lights off.

---

The first messages were ordinary. *How are you. Did you eat. I saw something today that reminded me of you.* He had photographed a woman selling plantain near a traffic light, holding an umbrella in the sun, and he had written beneath it: *She has your stubborn face. I told her she was beautiful.*

She laughed and then immediately started crying and could not tell which feeling came from which.

The middle messages were harder. *I know you're upset with me. I don't always know what I do wrong and that is its own kind of failure. I'm trying to learn how to ask better questions.* And then, a few days later: *I found a therapist. I haven't gone yet. I'm writing that here so there is a record of me saying it. Someone who will know if I don't follow through.*

She had been the record. And she hadn't been there to receive it.

---

The last message came at 6:14 in the morning of the day he died.

*Coming to you today. Surprise. Don't make plans. I want to walk through Osu with you and eat the bad jollof from that place you love. I want to hold your hand in public like we were twenty again. I've been practising what I want to say to you but I think I'll throw it all away when I see your face. That's always what happens with you. You make me forget my script. See you soon.*

---

Ama read it seven times.

She had kept her phone on silent that morning. She had been making coffee and listening to a podcast about productivity. He had been on the road, driving toward her with a speech in his pocket that he planned to forget.

Three hours between them. No time at all. Every moment since, immeasurable.

---

She started going to the grave on Thursdays.

Not to grieve — or not only to grieve. Mostly to talk. To say the things she would have said if she had picked up the phone, read the messages, met him at the door with open arms instead of an unlocked notification.

She told him about the jollof place. That it had closed down, actually. That she'd only pretended it was good because he'd been so proud of finding it.

She told him about the therapist. That she'd finally made an appointment. That she thought of it as finishing something he started.

She told him she had read all forty-seven messages and that she intended to keep reading them, regularly, on days when she forgot how to believe that love — even incomplete, even complicated, even conducted largely over distance and silences — was real.

That she was keeping them as a record of him following through.
