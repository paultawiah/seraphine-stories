On the third Sunday of every month, the women of the choir wore white.

Nana had worn white for eleven years. She owned five identical white blouses specifically for this purpose, which she washed and pressed with a devotion she sometimes thought exceeded her devotion to the faith itself. But faith, she had learned, was mostly practice. You showed up. You wore the right thing. You opened your mouth and let the sound come out. What you felt inside was between you and God and nobody's business.

"You look radiant this morning, Sister Nana." This was Auntie Efua, who said it every week and meant it kindly and did not know, could not know, that Nana had spent Saturday night sitting in her car outside her own house for forty-five minutes before she could make herself go inside.

"God has been good," Nana said. The correct answer. The true answer, she supposed, in the large sense — in the sense that she was alive and employed and her children were healthy and she had a car to sit in. She was not ungrateful for these things. She was simply also very, very tired.

---

The trouble with being known as a woman of faith was that it closed certain doors.

People did not ask certain questions when you were known as a prayer warrior, a pillar of the women's fellowship, the one who could always be counted on to provide the devotion at the end of meetings. They assumed your house was in order. They assumed your marriage was a testimony. They assumed the peace on your face was not, in fact, a performance you had perfected over a decade.

Nana had no language for what was actually happening because every language she had been given described something else. *I'm struggling* meant you needed prayer, and prayer meant people would come to your house and her husband would sit in the living room smiling and she would have to smile too and afterward they would say *see how the Lord is working in your home* and she would want to scream into a pillow.

*I'm not happy* was not a sentence a woman in her position said out loud. Not in this community, not with these stakes.

She had tried it once. With the pastor's wife, a woman she had trusted. She had chosen her words carefully, said only: *I wonder sometimes if I made the right choices.* A small, contained sentence. The pastor's wife had taken her hand and said: *God honors commitment. The enemy is a liar.* And that was that.

---

The thing about the women's fellowship was that they loved each other.

Nana held onto this. Even in the moments of most acute loneliness, she recognized that what surrounded her was genuine — the genuine concern of women who had learned the same language she had, who applied it with the best intentions they knew how to have, who would show up at her door with food and prayer at any hour of any day if she called them.

She wasn't alone. She just couldn't, exactly, be *known.*

There was a difference. She was still working out what to do with it.

---

Her daughter was seventeen and watching everything.

Nana could see it sometimes — the way Abena observed her mother's face in the seconds before it arranged itself into Sunday composure. Children who grow up watching their parents perform learn to read the backstage.

On a Tuesday evening, Abena came and sat beside her at the kitchen table and didn't say anything for a while. They stayed like that in the quiet, the two of them, in the particular stillness of an Accra evening when the traffic has finally settled.

Then Abena said: "Mama. Are you okay?"

Not fine. Not blessed. Okay. A smaller, more honest word.

Nana looked at her daughter — this person she had grown without fully understanding how — and felt something in her chest move.

"I'm working on it," she said.

It was the truest thing she had said in a long time.

---

She called a counsellor the following week. Not the church kind — though she did not think the church kind was wrong, only that she needed someone whose job was only to listen, not also to testify. A woman she found through a friend of a friend, with a small office near Labone and a face that asked nothing of you.

At the first session she sat in the chair and didn't know where to start.

"There's no right order," the counsellor said.

So Nana started in the middle, as people usually do. With a Sunday morning and a white blouse and a feeling she had been carrying long enough that she couldn't remember anymore what it felt like to set it down.

---

She still wore white on the third Sunday of every month.

She still said *God has been good* when Auntie Efua asked, because in the large sense it remained true, and she was not interested in the performance of suffering any more than she had been interested in the performance of serenity.

But something had shifted. She was learning to hold the complexity — the genuine faith and the genuine exhaustion, the real community and the real isolation, the love she had for the life she had built and the honest grief for the parts of herself that had been quietly shelved along the way.

She did not have a testimony about it yet. She wasn't sure she would.

Some things were just true without being turned into a lesson. Some growth happened in the silence between the singing.

She was learning that this was also a kind of faith.
